Last week: storm waivers. This week: should you actually buy the policy?

What comprehensive travel insurance costs in 2026, what Cancel For Any Reason really buys you, and why the card in your wallet usually is not enough on its own.

By Chris Bacon  ·  Founder, Vacation Math  ·  6 min read

Last week I walked through what airlines, cruise lines, and Disney actually do when a hurricane shows up. The short version: their protection is real, and it is also narrow. A lot of you emailed the same follow-up: okay, so should I just buy travel insurance?

This week is that answer — not the storm-specific fine print, the actual product. What a comprehensive policy costs in 2026, what Cancel For Any Reason really buys you, and why the travel card in your wallet is usually not a full substitute.

What you're actually buying

A comprehensive policy is built to protect the money you already spent and the medical bill if something goes wrong on the trip. Typical pieces, per Squaremouth:

  • Trip cancellation / interruption (usually 100% of insured prepaid, nonrefundable costs for listed reasons)
  • Emergency medical (often $50,000+ recommended minimum)
  • Medical evacuation (often $100,000+)
  • Travel delay and baggage limits on top

A medical-only plan skips protecting the trip cost and focuses on health abroad. That's cheaper, and it is the wrong tool if your risk is a $4,000 cruise deposit you can't get back.

On a $4,000 Disney or cruise trip, that 4%–10% band is roughly $160–$400 for comprehensive before any CFAR upgrade. The question is never “is $300 a lot?” It's “is $300 cheap relative to the nonrefundable chunk I'm protecting?”

CFAR: the flexibility last week's storm story was pointing at

Standard trip cancellation only pays for a short list of reasons (illness, death in the family, jury duty, and so on). A bad forecast that hasn't triggered an official warning usually isn't on that list — which is exactly the gap from Issue #14.

Cancel For Any Reason is the add-on that closes it, with three catches Squaremouth and NerdWallet both hammer:

  1. It reimburses 50%–75% of prepaid nonrefundable costs (most plans land at 75%), not 100%.
  2. It raises the premium about 40%–60% (roughly an extra 3% of trip cost).
  3. You almost always have to buy it within 14–21 days of your first trip deposit. Miss that window and CFAR is gone for that trip at any price.

If you want the option to walk away because you're uneasy — not because a listed event happened — CFAR is the product. Everything else is a named-peril policy with nicer marketing.

Your credit card is not a comprehensive policy

Premium cards (Chase Sapphire Reserve, Amex Platinum, and friends) are useful. They are also incomplete.

Squaremouth's plain version: even with Sapphire Reserve or Amex Platinum, you will likely still want a separate policy for expensive or international trips. The card is a floor. It is not the ceiling.

A simple decision rule

01
Refundable domestic weekend, small spend? Card benefits are probably enough. Don't buy a $200 policy to protect a $180 hotel you can cancel.
02
Big prepaid nonrefundable chunk (cruise, Disney package, international airfare)? Price a comprehensive policy. Use 4%–10% of that prepaid total as the gut check.
03
You want to cancel for reasons that aren't on the list? That's CFAR — and the clock started at your deposit, not at the week you got nervous.
04
International, or any real medical risk abroad? Do not lean on a $2,500 card medical limit. Buy medical coverage with limits that match the destination.
