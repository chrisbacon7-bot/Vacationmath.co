The Post-July 4th Price Crash That Isn't Happening This Year

Every year I tell people the same thing: wait a week after July 4th and prices drop. This year the math doesn't work that way — here's why, and where the real savings actually are.

By Chris Bacon  ·  Founder, Vacation Math  ·  6 min read

Every year around this time, I tell people the same thing: wait a week after July 4th and prices drop. That's usually true — airlines and hotels see demand fall off a cliff the day after the holiday, so they cut prices to fill seats and rooms.

This year the math doesn't work that way, and I wanted to show you why instead of just telling you.

Why the usual crash didn't happen

01
The crowds were record-sized, not shrinking. AAA's July 4th travel forecast put holiday-week travel at 72.2 million Americans, the highest on record and up from 71.8 million last year. About 61.4 million of those trips — roughly 85% — were by car. That's not a market about to go quiet.
02
Airlines pulled seats instead of prices. Domestic flights during the holiday period averaged around $830 a ticket, with fares to some top destinations like Chicago and Denver running about 5% higher than last year. Airlines have been trimming capacity rather than discounting to fill planes — the opposite of what usually pushes prices down after a holiday peak.
03
The government's own numbers back it up. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' CPI data showed airline fares up sharply on both a monthly and year-over-year basis heading into summer 2026, and car rentals running well above last summer's levels. None of this looks like a market getting ready to discount.
04
Gas is actually the one thing giving relief. AAA's national gas average has pulled back meaningfully from its spring peak — real relief at the pump, but relief from a spike, not from normal. It's still running well above this time last year.
