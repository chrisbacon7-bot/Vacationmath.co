Mexico Doubled Its Cruise Fee and You Will Never See It on Your Invoice

A cost on your next Western Caribbean sailing went up 100% on August 1. It is bundled into taxes and fees, it is scheduled to go up twice more, and almost nobody booking right now knows it exists.

By Chris Bacon  ·  Founder, Vacation Math  ·  6 min read

A cost on your next Western Caribbean sailing went up 100% on August 1. It is bundled into taxes and fees, it is scheduled to go up twice more, and almost nobody booking right now knows it exists.

This is the kind of thing I watch for, because it is exactly how a cruise gets more expensive without the fare ever changing.

What actually changed on August 1

Mexico charges foreign cruise passengers a federal duty called the Derecho de No Residente. On August 1, 2026, it doubled from $5 to $10 per person. It applies to anyone on a ship that calls at a Mexican port, which covers Cozumel, Costa Maya, Progreso, Cabo San Lucas, Puerto Vallarta, Mazatlan, and Ensenada.

You pay it once per itinerary, not once per port. A Western Caribbean sailing that stops in both Cozumel and Costa Maya is still one charge.

The number applies based on your sailing date, not your booking date. If you booked in March for an October cruise, you are paying $10.

Why it never shows up as a line item

Cruise lines collect this in advance, folded into either the fare or the taxes and fees line on your booking. You do not hand anyone $10 at the pier. Some lines break it out as a separate duty and most simply absorb it into the tax total, which is why a fee that doubled generated almost no passenger reaction.

That is worth sitting with. The cost is real, it is on your invoice, and the invoice is built in a way that makes it invisible.

Mexico originally proposed $42 per passenger. The Florida-Caribbean Cruise Association argued that would make Mexican ports 213% more expensive than the Caribbean average, and the phased schedule above is the compromise that came out of it. The money is earmarked for port infrastructure, including a fourth pier at Cozumel.

What it costs a family of four

Forty dollars this year, up from twenty. On a $4,000 cruise that is rounding error, and I want to be straight about that rather than inflate it into something it is not.

The reason to track it is the direction. At $21 in 2028, the same family pays $84, and that is before you count what the ports themselves charge. It is a line that only moves one way, on a published schedule, and the schedule runs four years out.

The other reason is that it is not the only fee doing this. Cruise lines raised daily gratuities six times this year. Port fees have been drifting up across the Caribbean. Individually each one is small. Together they are the reason your all-in number keeps beating your fare estimate.

It is big enough to move ships

One detail tells you this matters more to the industry than the dollar figure suggests. Royal Caribbean is reported to be pulling two ships from West Coast deployments following the increase, where Mexican ports are most of the itinerary and there is nowhere else nearby to send them.

If a $5 per-passenger change can redraw a deployment map, it is not really a $5 change.

Four things to actually do before you book

01
Read the taxes and fees line, not just the fare. Two cruises with identical fares can differ by $100 or more for a family once port charges and duties are added. The comparison you are making at the fare level is not the comparison that hits your card.
02
Assume Mexican itineraries carry the duty. If any port on the map is in Mexico, add $10 per person to your mental total. For a family of four sailing in 2027, make that $15 each.
03
Ask whether your fare is guaranteed against fee increases. Most lines reserve the right to pass through new government charges even after you have paid in full. It is in the ticket contract. Worth knowing before you assume your total is locked.
04
Run the whole number before you put down a deposit. The fare is the part everyone compares. Gratuities, duties, drinks, and getting to the port are the part that decides what the trip actually costs.
